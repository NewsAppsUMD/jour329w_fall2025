## 1. County Overview  

Talbot County’s resident count stands at **37,663** (2022 ACS). The median age is **51.4 years**, reflecting an older population than the state average. Gender distribution is roughly even, with **18,023 males** and **19,640 females**. Racial‑ethnic composition is predominantly White (**≈78 %**, 29,600 people); Black residents number **4,593** (≈12 %); and Hispanic/Latino residents total **2,744** (≈7 %).  

Economically, the county reports a **median household income of $81,667**, well above the Maryland statewide median. The labor force comprises **17,654 workers**, of whom **559 are unemployed**, yielding an unemployment rate of roughly **3.2 %**. Poverty affects **9.3 %** of residents (≈3,460 people).  

Housing is characterized by a high ownership rate (**72.9 %**). The **median home value** is **$382,000**, while the **median monthly rent** is **$1,204**. However, **42.7 %** of renters are cost‑burdened (spending ≥30 % of income on rent).  

Broadband penetration is relatively strong at **88.1 %** of households, and the **uninsured rate** is low at **2.5 %**, indicating broad health‑coverage access.  

Educational attainment shows **6,565** residents holding a bachelor’s degree, **3,227** with a master’s, **1,424** with professional degrees, and **649** with doctorates. These figures point to a modestly educated workforce relative to the county’s income level.

## 3. Key Policy Areas & Challenges  

### Infrastructure – Water, Sewer, and Roads  

- **Issue:** Aging wastewater and water distribution systems require extensions to accommodate growth, while road assets suffer from reactive maintenance.  
- **Budget/Data Context:** FY 2026 allocates **$4.07 M** to roads & infrastructure (3.9 % of the General Fund) and **$5 M** within the Capital Projects Program for a water/sewer extension. The County’s total debt service is modest (**$2.59 M**, 1.6 % of the budget).  
- **Recent Developments:** Resolution 235 authorized a **$7.02 M** bond for a Route 33 sewer extension (unanimous vote). Emergency approval of a **$1.38 M** culvert repair on Chapel Road occurred without competitive bidding.  
- **Tensions/Trade‑offs:** Funding new extensions relies on user fees that will affect future household utility costs, while limited operating reserves constrain the ability to pre‑emptively replace aging road assets.  

### Housing Affordability & Rental Burden  

- **Issue:** High homeownership rates coexist with a **42.7 %** renter cost‑burden, indicating affordability stress for non‑owners.  
- **Budget/Data Context:** The General Fund does not earmark a dedicated housing‑affordability line item; however, the **Sanitary Districts Enterprise Fund** (budget **$7.7 M**) levies rates that indirectly affect housing costs.  
- **Recent Developments:** No specific housing‑affordability programs appear in FY 2026; meeting minutes show no legislative action on rent control or affordable‑housing incentives during the covered period.  
- **Tensions/Trade‑offs:** Balancing property‑tax revenue needs (which fund the majority of the budget, 42.6 %) with the desire to keep rental rates affordable creates pressure on the County to consider subsidies or incentive programs, none of which are presently funded.  

### Public Safety & Emergency Services  

- **Issue:** Maintaining adequate staffing and equipment for the Sheriff’s Office, detention center, and emergency medical services.  
- **Budget/Data Context:** Sheriff/Public Safety receives **$15.24 M** (9.5 % of the budget); Emergency Services receive **$11.51 M** (7.1 %). The Detention Center budget is **$7.56 M** (4.7 %).  
- **Recent Developments:** The FY 2026 budget includes a **$1.3 M** allocation for 911 technology and CAD replacement, reflecting a modernization priority. No major policy shifts were noted in the meeting minutes.  
- **Tensions/Trade‑offs:** Capital spending on technology competes with operating costs for staffing; any increase in personnel costs would require either higher tax rates or reallocation from other departments.  

### Education Funding  

- **Issue:** Ensuring stable financing for K‑12 schools and Chesapeake College amid a high per‑pupil cost structure.  
- **Budget/Data Context:** The Board of Education receives **$61.45 M** (38.1 % of the General Fund), the single largest allocation. Teacher pensions are a line item (**$0.44 M**) and school‑related debt service (**$5.3 M**).  
- **Recent Developments:** FY 2026 includes a **$2.0 M** appropriation to Chesapeake College, and modest capital improvements (**$260.5 k**) for public schools.  
- **Tensions/Trade‑offs:** Education’s share of the budget leaves limited flexibility for other priorities; any increase in education spending would pressure the county to raise property taxes or draw deeper from reserves.  

### Healthcare & Social Services (Senior Homelessness)  

- **Issue:** A growing senior‑homelessness crisis in a county where **≈30 %** of residents are 65 + (the highest share in Maryland).  
- **Budget/Data Context:** Health Department receives **$3.11 M** (1.9 % of the budget). No dedicated line for senior services appears in FY 2026.  
- **Recent Developments:** The County Council established a **Senior Citizens Task Force** (expanded to 11 members) after criticism of fragmented services. Public testimony highlighted gaps between Social Security benefits (**$2,008/mo**) and basic living costs (**$3,387/mo**).  
- **Tensions/Trade‑offs:** Funding senior‑specific programs would likely require reallocating from other social‑service lines or increasing tax/fee revenues; the current budget does not earmark new resources for this demographic.
