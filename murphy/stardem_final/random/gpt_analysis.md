# Blueprint Funding Trends for Eastern Shore School Districts

## Overview of Per-Pupil Funding Trends (FY2020–FY2025)

Maryland’s **Blueprint for Maryland’s Future** – a sweeping 10-year education reform passed in 2021 – has significantly altered school funding patterns across the state. Under the Blueprint, state aid for education has been rising, especially for districts with higher needs or less local wealth, while also gradually requiring counties to contribute their “local share” of all funding formulas. The figures from the Department of Legislative Services show per-pupil funding trends from FY2020 to FY2024, as well as the state/local breakdown for FY2025, for each county.

This analysis focuses on five Eastern Shore counties – **Dorchester, Talbot, Queen Anne’s, Caroline, and Kent** – highlighting how per-student funding has changed in recent years and the Blueprint context behind these changes.

---

## Dorchester County

Dorchester County’s per-pupil funding is among the highest in Maryland, driven by substantial state aid. State funding for Dorchester rose from about **$11,996 in FY2020** to **$14,405 in FY2024**, an increase of roughly **37% since FY2019**, one of the largest jumps in the state. Local per-pupil funding also grew, from **$4,432 to $5,698** over FY2020–FY2024, as the county gradually increased its own contributions.

By FY2025, Dorchester’s total per-student funding reached roughly **$21,000**, with the state providing about **74%** of that amount. This reflects the Blueprint’s equity-focused formula, which directs large shares of state funding to lower-wealth districts.

Despite these funding gains, Dorchester has struggled to meet Blueprint mandates. Requirements to raise teacher salaries (including a **$60,000 starting salary target**) and expand programs have increased the county’s share of costs and created budget pressure. In FY2025, the school district initially faced a deficit exceeding **$10 million**, later reduced to **$5.5 million** through cost-cutting measures. School leaders warned that pausing pay raises would jeopardize compliance with state-mandated salary targets and risk penalties.

County and school officials have discussed seeking state waivers or flexibility, arguing that rural counties like Dorchester face unique constraints due to limited tax bases. State policy experts have acknowledged these challenges and suggested mechanisms such as increased local revenue authority to help counties meet Blueprint obligations. Overall, Dorchester’s funding has risen sharply under the Blueprint, but translating those dollars into sustainable compliance has required difficult fiscal choices.

---

## Talbot County

Talbot County exemplifies a high-wealth district with a small student population. It receives the lowest state aid share in Maryland: in FY2025, only about **33%** of its roughly **$19,238 per-pupil funding** came from the state. Even so, the Blueprint significantly increased Talbot’s state aid in recent years.

State per-pupil funding for Talbot grew **49% from FY2019 to FY2024**, the largest increase of any district in percentage terms, rising from **$4,579 in FY2020** to **$5,883 in FY2024**. Local funding remains the dominant share of Talbot’s school budget and increased from **$9,568 to $12,047 per pupil** over the same period. Total funding per student rose about **27% since FY2020**, reaching roughly **$18,000–$19,000**.

Talbot has used Blueprint funds to invest in teacher recruitment, diversity initiatives, and college and career readiness. State reviewers praised the district’s partnerships with local colleges and its data-driven instructional strategies. However, Talbot faces challenges tied to its small scale. Expanding early childhood programs or specialized coursework is difficult with limited staff and enrollment, and new state minimum school funding rules can strain small schools with high fixed costs.

District leaders have carefully reallocated expenditures by program and school and sought waivers for interim Blueprint targets that are difficult to meet in a small district context. Talbot’s experience illustrates how Blueprint-driven funding growth can still require significant administrative adaptation in districts with small enrollments and high local funding shares.

---

## Queen Anne’s County

Queen Anne’s County has seen relatively modest funding growth under the Blueprint compared with other Maryland districts. State aid increased only about **2% in real terms from FY2019 to FY2024**, the smallest gain statewide. State per-pupil funding rose from **$5,844 in FY2020** to **$6,926 in FY2024**, a comparatively small increase.

As a result, Queen Anne’s remains near the bottom in total funding per pupil, at about **$17,662 in FY2025**, ranking **23rd of 24 districts**. Local funding has had to compensate, increasing from **$7,934 to $9,714 per pupil** between FY2020 and FY2024. Even with these increases, the state provides only about **40%** of school funding, leaving roughly **60%** to local taxpayers.

This funding structure has put sustained pressure on Queen Anne’s County Public Schools. Blueprint mandates – including salary increases, expanded pre-K, and additional supports for at-risk students – have driven costs upward faster than state aid. District officials have warned of multi-million-dollar budget shortfalls in future years without additional county or state support.

In recent years, the district has relied on measures such as staff furloughs, including a four-day furlough that saved approximately **$1.4 million**, to balance the budget. Local contributions have increased by roughly **29% over three years**, but officials caution that such growth may not be sustainable. Queen Anne’s illustrates how limited state growth under the Blueprint can force difficult local decisions even as program requirements expand.

## Caroline County

Caroline County, a low-wealth jurisdiction, has benefited substantially from Blueprint-driven state funding increases. State per-pupil aid rose by about **40% from FY2019 to FY2024**, the **fifth-largest increase** in Maryland. State aid increased from **$12,432 per student in FY2020** to **$15,287 in FY2024**.

Because of its limited tax base, Caroline’s local contribution is small, increasing only slightly from **$2,760 per pupil in FY2020** to **$3,002 in FY2024**, and accounting for just **18% of total funding** in FY2025. The state provides more than **80%** of Caroline’s approximately **$19,796 per-pupil funding**, reflecting the Blueprint’s equity goals.

With this influx of state support, Caroline has expanded early childhood education, developed comprehensive pre-K plans, and invested in teacher recruitment and professional development. The district has also been recognized for strong curriculum planning aligned with Blueprint priorities in literacy and math.

Caroline’s primary challenges are administrative capacity and compliance rather than raising local funds. Implementing new teacher career ladders, meeting aggressive timelines, and collecting required performance data strain the resources of a small rural district. Nonetheless, Caroline’s experience demonstrates the Blueprint’s intended impact: enabling substantial educational investments in districts that previously lacked the means to fund them locally.

---

## Kent County

Kent County is Maryland’s smallest school district by enrollment, and the Blueprint has affected it in distinctive ways. Per-pupil funding is relatively high – about **$21,188 in FY2025**, sixth highest statewide – due to moderate state aid combined with very high local funding spread across a small student population.

State per-pupil aid increased by roughly **33.8% from FY2019 to FY2024**, rising from about **$6,666 in FY2020** to **$7,957 in FY2024**. Local funding, already among the highest in the state, increased from **$10,058 to $11,912 per pupil** over the same period. Although the state provides only around **40%** of total funding, Kent’s strong local effort results in high overall per-student spending.

Despite this, Kent has faced significant budget strain. Declining enrollment leaves the district with excess capacity and high fixed costs, while Blueprint mandates add further pressure. In FY2025, the district initially proposed eliminating **44 positions** to close a deficit. County commissioners intervened with an additional **$300,000**, reducing planned layoffs to **18 positions**, though those cuts are scheduled to occur by 2026.

Kent officials estimate that Blueprint obligations will increase local costs by roughly **$8 million through 2030**, a major burden for a small tax base. While the district benefits from the ability to target student needs closely, maintaining a full slate of required programs with very small enrollments remains an ongoing challenge.

---

## Conclusion

Across Dorchester, Talbot, Queen Anne’s, Caroline, and Kent counties, the Blueprint for Maryland’s Future has driven significant increases in education funding, particularly state aid. Low-wealth counties such as Dorchester and Caroline experienced the largest state-driven gains, enabling major program expansions but also introducing new fiscal pressures. Wealthier or smaller counties, including Talbot, Queen Anne’s, and Kent, received more limited state growth, making local funding decisions and financial management central to Blueprint implementation.

All five counties are investing more per student than they were prior to the Blueprint, reflecting the scale and ambition of Maryland’s education reform. Whether these investments translate into sustained improvements will depend on how effectively the state addresses local implementation challenges and ensures long-term fiscal sustainability across diverse county contexts.
