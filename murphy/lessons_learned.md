1. **How has your understanding of what LLMs are useful for in a journalism context changed during this class?**

Truthfully, I don’t know that my understanding has changed in the sense that I thought one thing and now I think something else. It’s more that I had a good sense of what LLMs shouldn’t be used for in journalism, but I didn’t necessarily know what they should or could be used for. I had a vague sense of “I’m sure LLMs could be useful,” but it’s not like I had a list of things I thought it would be useful for. This class has done two things: first, it’s solidified my understanding of what LLMs should never, under any circumstance, be used to do. And honestly, in general, I think this includes text generation — on the front end, at least. I’m appalled by the idea of an “AI-assisted reporter.” Because while I was never particularly convinced that AI was going to “replace” journalists, I’m now almost positive it won’t. It just requires so much human oversight — even more than I thought prior to taking this class. At the same time, this class has taught me where AI does fit into journalism: the back end. In other words, I think LLMs are going to allow journalists to do things they’ve never been able to before — stories that either required too many people, too much time or both. But the key piece I’ve learned here is that while AI is incredibly helpful for admin tasks, building web scrapers and summarizing documents, this class has taught me just how infallible LLMs are, and how necessary it is for a human to be in the loop.

2. **What are the biggest problems you've had using LLMs for the work in this class?**

The steepest learning curve was the balance between prompting and context. It became apparent toward the end of the semester — roughly mid-November or so — that no prompt alone, regardless of quality, could adequately safeguard against hallucinations and other errors in LLM-generated text. Granted, nothing can reliably protect against that, but my point is that feeding an LLM only the prompt — and in our case the story data — is an invitation for wild AI hallucinations. The lesson there was that LLMs not only require ultra-curated prompts but also immense amounts of supplementary information independent of the story data itself. Another major challenge, somewhat similarly, was learning how best to provide context to an LLM — not only what to provide but also how to get it and how to format it. This applies to the story data itself, too; if I had to guess, the LLMs we had access to could analyze the Star-Democrat’s raw story data with, at most, 40% accuracy. Despite the wealth of information, the inclusion of irrelevant articles, the general disorganization and the lack of actual metadata made any AI-generated analysis of the original story data effectively meaningless. But that meant figuring out how to generate good metadata, how to classify articles based on said metadata and how to compile only articles relevant to any given topic. Then, it’s a matter of figuring out what other information an LLM would need to have to generate a meaningful analysis. Oh, and do all that without getting rate-limited or hitting your daily token limit.

3. **What specific advice would you give to fellow journalism students about using LLMs to understand news stories or archives?**
    1. *Get an idea of what you’re working with.* In order for an LLM to analyze content, it has to know what it’s analyzing — which means you need to know what it’s analyzing. This doesn’t require knowing the ins and outs of every story; actually, it’s more important to understand the types of stories in your archive, the date range of your stories, the format of your story data, any inconsistencies or structural flaws within your dataset, etc.
    2. *Try out different prompts and test different models.*There’s no such thing as a perfect prompt, but some will work better than others. Try using shorter prompts, or more specific ones; use different words, set different rules. Also, every model has its pros and cons. See how different models — and different families of models — react to different prompts, and think about how you can split up tasks to take advantage of different models’ strengths.
    3. *Use LLMs to help you use LLMs.* No one knows how to write LLM prompts better than an LLM itself. You can also use them to scrape webpages and generate Markdown summaries of documents. It’s also not a bad idea to try bouncing ideas off of an LLM or asking one for its “thoughts.” 
    4. *If you’re trying to generate a beat book, prioritize getting good metadata, then go from there.* The actual story text is important, but having an LLM parse hundreds of articles in full, in their raw form, all at once won’t produce great results. Your best bet is to pass each story to an LLM and have it classify it and extract metadata. This could be topics, themes, people, places, agencies, etc. In any case, good metadata — and by good, I mean comprehensive, consistent, structured metadata — is the foundation of a good beat book.
    5. *Give the LLM more than just the stories to work with.* News articles will never have every detail. They will reference data points without explaining or contextualizing the entire dataset, or briefly mention concepts and metrics that are probably well-known among the publication’s readers but are otherwise completely meaningless. Go get that data — scrape it, summarize it, etc., and feed it to the LLM alongside the story data to contextualize the stories and fill in any gaps.
