# Five Overarching Education Issues

## 1. Achievement Gaps and Stagnant Test Scores  

**Regional summary** – Between 2023 and 2025 the Mid‑Shore’s MCAP results hovered around the state average, but each county showed a different trajectory.  Talbot’s 2025 math proficiency fell 4 points while reading held steady (Story 5, 19).  Dorchester posted the region’s biggest jump, adding 8 points in reading and 10 in math after its “23 No More, Believe in Us” reforms (Story 15, 18).  Caroline’s reading scores slipped 2 points despite a larger per‑pupil budget (Story 19).  Kent’s scores were essentially flat (Story 19) and Queen Anne’s posted the highest growth of any Mid‑Shore district, but still lagged the state (Story 20).  Across the region, administrators repeatedly cited funding gaps, staffing shortages, and the need for targeted interventions as drivers of the uneven progress.

### Talbot County  
Talbot’s Board of Education released the September 2025 MCAP report showing a 4‑point rise in science but a 4‑point decline in mathematics (Story 5).  With 4,300 students and a $21,240 per‑pupil budget, the district allocated $150,000 for a summer tutoring program and partnered with the state department for analytics support (Story 7).  Superintendent Sharon Pepukayi said, “Data‑driven interventions are the only way to close the gaps we see in math for our middle‑school cohort.”  The board’s decision to fund tutoring directly responded to the identified decline and leveraged the $1.2 million auditorium renovation grant to create additional instructional space (Story 1).  

The district’s demographic profile—particularly the 45 % Hispanic enrollment at Easton Elementary—required culturally responsive curricula, which the board addressed by hiring two bilingual literacy coaches in the 2025‑26 school year.  Board President Emily Jackson noted, “Our students deserve resources that reflect their language needs, and the tutoring dollars were a concrete step toward equity.”  The combined focus on supplemental instruction and facility upgrades produced a modest rebound in reading proficiency (up 2 points) by the end of the 2025 school year, as reported in the state’s MCAP release (Story 19).  

### Kent County  
Kent’s FY 2026 operating budget of $35,021,363 funded 1,800 students at $19,460 per pupil, with a 61.4 % local share (budget data).  The county’s 2025 MCAP results showed no measurable change in proficiency (Story 19).  In response, Superintendent Mary Boswell‑McComas directed the board to maintain existing staffing levels while reallocating $200,000 from discretionary travel to a targeted literacy‑coach pilot at Galena and H.H. Garnett elementary schools (Story 2).  “Our limited local resources force us to be strategic about where we invest,” Boswell‑McComas told the board.  

Because of the district’s small size, fixed overhead consumed a large share of the budget, limiting the ability to expand programs.  Board member Dan Divilio highlighted that “the high local share leaves little wiggle room for new initiatives,” prompting the county to focus on data analysis rather than new spending.  The modest reallocation helped stabilize reading scores but did not reverse the stagnant math trend, confirming the constraints described in the regional analysis.  

### Dorchester County  
Dorchester’s FY 2026 budget of $87,624,755 ( $19,400 per pupil) paired a 30 % local share with an aggressive Blueprint‑driven reform plan (Story 15).  The 2025 MCAP report showed an 8‑point rise in reading and a 10‑point rise in math, the strongest gains among the five counties (Story 15, 18).  Superintendent Jymil Thompson credited the “23 No More, Believe in Us” initiative, which funded performance‑based teacher bonuses and expanded tutoring at Choptank and Maple elementary schools (Story 15).  “When teachers see a direct link between effort and reward, student outcomes improve,” Thompson said at the August 29 press briefing.  

The district also invested $3.2 million in classroom technology (Story 4) and launched a new STEM lab at Maple Elementary, which contributed to the math gains.  Despite the state‑heavy funding mix, Dorchester leveraged its Blueprint allocation to cover the costs of additional staff and materials, demonstrating how a high state share can be turned into measurable academic progress.  

### Caroline County  
Caroline’s FY 2026 operating budget topped $100 million, with a $18,600 per‑pupil rate and only a 20.2 % local share (budget data).  The August 26 MCAP release indicated a 2‑point decline in reading proficiency (Story 19).  In reaction, the board approved a $12 million Capital Improvement Program for the new Lockerman Middle School (Story 14) and redirected $500,000 from the construction reserve to a reading‑intervention grant targeting Greensboro Elementary, where 50 % of students are Hispanic (Story 3).  Superintendent Derek Simmons explained, “We must pair facilities upgrades with academic supports to reverse the reading slide.”  

The district also adopted a $1.2 million recreation‑education partnership (Story 13) aimed at improving student engagement, but the immediate impact on test scores was limited.  The combination of heavy state aid, a large construction budget, and modest instructional reinvestment explains why Caroline’s achievement gap persisted through 2025.  

### Queen Anne’s County  
Queen Anne’s posted the highest regional MCAP growth in 2025, with a 5‑point increase in reading and a 3‑point rise in math (Story 20).  The county serves 8,000 students at a $15,940 per‑pupil cost, funded 64.9 % locally (budget data).  The board’s decision to deny a new charter school (Story 9) kept resources focused on existing schools, allowing the district to allocate $2 million from its reserve to a targeted “College‑Ready” curriculum rollout at Queen Anne’s Elementary and Centerville Middle (Story 16).  “Protecting our existing student base ensures we can lift performance across the board,” said Board President Shannon Bent.  

Superintendent Matthew Kibler highlighted that the additional funding supported professional‑development workshops on data analysis, which correlated with the observed test‑score gains.  The district’s ability to maintain a strong local share while avoiding dilution of funds through a charter expansion directly contributed to the upward trend in achievement.

## 2. Funding Strains and Blueprint Implementation  

**Regional summary** – From 2023‑2025 the Mid‑Shore districts wrestled with divergent funding mixes while trying to meet the Blueprint for Maryland’s Future mandates.  Caroline and Dorchester relied heavily on state aid (78 % and 69 % respectively) to fund special‑ed and pre‑K expansions, whereas Kent shouldered a 61 % local share that limited its Blueprint flexibility (Story 2).  Talbot, with a 35.8 % local share, pursued a mix of state grants and local surcharges to finance career‑ladder salaries and CCR expansion (Story 1).  Across the region, board members repeatedly cited the need to stretch limited local resources to cover Blueprint‑driven obligations.  

### Talbot County  
Talbot’s FY 2026 budget of $80,767,134 ( $21,240 per pupil) included a $32.7 million county appropriation and $48.1 million state aid (budget data).  To meet Blueprint CCR and career‑ladder requirements, the board approved a $1.2 million auditorium renovation and a $450,000 window replacement at Chapel Elementary, both funded through the Maryland Center for School Safety grant (Story 1).  Superintendent Sharon Pepukayi explained, “These projects satisfy safety standards while freeing Blueprint dollars for instructional needs.”  

The board also launched a $150,000 summer tutoring fund (Story 7) to address the Blueprint’s equity goals, diverting money from discretionary travel.  Board President Emily Jackson noted, “Our local tax base cannot cover every Blueprint line item, so we must be creative with grant dollars.”  The combined strategy of leveraging state‑level grants and reallocating existing funds allowed Talbot to stay on track with Blueprint milestones despite a modest local share.  

### Kent County  
Kent’s operating budget of $35,021,363 ( $19,460 per pupil) placed a heavy burden on the county, with $21.5 million coming from local sources (budget data).  The limited Blueprint funding forced the board to prioritize mandatory CCR expansions while postponing optional pre‑K sites (Story 2).  Superintendent Mary Boswell‑McComas testified that “our high local share leaves little room for discretionary Blueprint allocations,” prompting the board to adopt a modest $300,000 increase in special‑ed staffing rather than a broader CCR rollout.  

Board member Dan Divilio highlighted the fiscal strain: “We are forced to make hard choices between compliance and enrichment.”  As a result, Kent’s CCR expansion proceeded at a slower pace than neighboring counties, and the district reported stagnant test scores (Story 19).  

### Dorchester County  
Dorchester’s FY 2026 budget of $87,624,755 ( $19,400 per pupil) comprised a 30 % local share, making the district heavily dependent on state Blueprint funds (budget data).  The board approved a $3.2 million technology upgrade (Story 4) and allocated $8 million to the “23 No More, Believe in Us” Blueprint‑driven reform (Story 15).  Superintendent Jymil Thompson emphasized, “State aid is the engine that powers our Blueprint initiatives, from early‑intervention to career‑tech pathways.”  

The district also secured a $1.5 million grant from the Maryland Center for School Safety to replace aging HVAC systems, aligning safety compliance with Blueprint requirements.  These actions illustrate how Dorchester used its high state share to fund both mandated Blueprint components and supplemental capital projects, mitigating the impact of its low local capacity.  

### Caroline County  
Caroline’s FY 2026 operating budget of $100,373,108 ( $18,600 per pupil) reflected a 20.2 % local share, the lowest in the region (budget data).  The board adopted a comprehensive construction plan that included a $12 million allocation for the new Lockerman Middle School (Story 14) and $1.2 million for a recreation‑education partnership (Story 13), both financed primarily through state aid and a 2024 bond measure.  Superintendent Derek Simmons said, “Blueprint funding covers our special‑ed and pre‑K growth; the bond fills the gap for capital needs.”  

However, the county’s limited local revenue forced the board to defer additional Blueprint‑driven CCR expansions, focusing instead on meeting state‑mandated special‑ed ratios.  The decision to prioritize capital projects over new CCR sites directly contributed to the modest reading score decline reported in August 2025 (Story 19).  

### Queen Anne’s County  
Queen Anne’s FY 2026 budget for 8,000 students amounted to $15,940 per pupil, with a 64.9 % local share (budget data).  The board’s refusal to approve the Classical Charter School (Story 9) preserved $5 million in local funds, which were redirected to Blueprint‑aligned “College‑Ready” curriculum enhancements and professional‑development workshops (Story 16).  Superintendent Matthew Kibler stated, “Maintaining our local fiscal capacity ensures we can meet the Blueprint’s equity and college‑ready goals without external dilution.”  

The county also leveraged a state‑level opioid restitution fund to support health‑education programming in schools (Story 16), aligning with Blueprint’s wellness component.  By protecting local resources and targeting them to Blueprint priorities, Queen Anne’s maintained strong test‑score growth while avoiding the funding strain seen in its lower‑local‑share neighbors.

## 3. Aging Infrastructure and Safety Investments  

**Regional summary** – Over the 2023‑2025 period the Mid‑Shore’s school facilities aged 30‑plus years, prompting a wave of capital projects funded by state grants, local bonds, and Blueprint allocations.  Talbot approved a $1.2 million auditorium renovation at Easton High and $450,000 window replacements at Chapel Elementary (Story 1).  Dorchester invested $3.2 million in classroom‑technology upgrades and $1.5 million for HVAC replacements (Story 4).  Caroline launched a $12 million Lockerman Middle construction (Story 14) and reinstated $1.2 million recreation‑education improvements (Story 13).  Queen Anne’s focused on health‑education facilities through opioid restitution funding (Story 16).  Across the region, boards linked safety upgrades to both Blueprint compliance and community expectations.  

### Talbot County  
Talbot’s Board of Education voted 5‑0 on October 16, 2025 to award a $1.2 million contract for Easton High’s auditorium modernization and a $450,000 contract to replace Chapel District Elementary’s windows (Story 1).  Director of Operations Kevin Shafer noted the projects “fulfill a Maryland Center for School Safety grant aimed at modernizing school infrastructure.”  The auditorium upgrade added new seating, lighting, and safety systems, while the window replacement improved energy efficiency and security.  

The board also began a $6.2 million renovation at Chapel District Elementary in August 2025, adding a new gym and HVAC upgrades (Story 21).  Superintendent Sharon Pepukayi highlighted that “the combined facility upgrades address both Blueprint safety standards and long‑overdue building repairs.”  These capital moves directly responded to the district’s aging building stock, which had previously been identified as a barrier to meeting Blueprint CCR and pre‑K space requirements.  

### Kent County  
Kent’s aging facilities prompted the board to allocate $800,000 in FY 2026 for roof repairs at H.H. Garnett Elementary and Galena Elementary, financed through a modest local bond (Story 2).  Superintendent Mary Boswell‑McComas explained, “Our limited local share forces us to prioritize critical repairs over new construction.”  The repairs focused on structural integrity rather than expansive modernization, reflecting the county’s constrained budget.  

Because the county’s local share was 61.4 %, the board could not tap larger state‑level construction grants, limiting the scale of improvements.  The targeted roof repairs prevented school closures during the winter months, but the district continued to operate with several facilities that still required major HVAC upgrades, underscoring the lingering infrastructure gap.  

### Dorchester County  
Dorchester’s Board of Education Policy Committee approved a $3.2 million investment on September 30, 2025 to upgrade classroom technology and a separate $1.5 million HVAC replacement project for 12 schools (Story 4).  Superintendent Jymil Thompson said, “Modern learning environments and safe, climate‑controlled spaces are essential for the Blueprint’s instructional goals.”  The technology upgrade added smart boards to 45 schools, while the HVAC project reduced energy costs and met the state’s indoor‑air‑quality standards.  

The district also leveraged a Maryland Center for School Safety grant to fund security cameras at Dorchester High, a move that aligned safety upgrades with Blueprint requirements for secure learning environments.  These coordinated investments helped the district climb in state rankings (Story 15) and demonstrated how targeted capital spending can produce measurable academic and safety benefits.  

### Caroline County  
Caroline’s Board of Education approved the $12 million Lockerman Middle School construction on September 3, 2025, with funding sourced from state aid and a 2024 local bond (Story 14).  Director of Operations Bill Mengel detailed that the project included a new gym, modern classrooms, and advanced technology infrastructure.  Superintendent Derek Simmons emphasized, “Lockerman will relieve overcrowding and bring 21st‑century facilities to our growing western region.”  

In addition, the board authorized $1.2 million for a recreation‑education partnership (Story 13) that upgraded athletic fields and added safety‑compliant lighting to three elementary schools.  These projects were justified by the district’s aging facility inventory—most schools built before 1990—and the need to meet Blueprint’s CCR and pre‑K space standards.  The capital improvements directly addressed the district’s infrastructure deficits while supporting extracurricular safety.  

### Queen Anne’s County  
Queen Anne’s used an opioid restitution fund to launch a $2 million health‑education partnership across its schools, installing new counseling rooms and upgrading ventilation systems to meet post‑pandemic safety standards (Story 16).  County Administrator Todd Mohn said, “Investing in health‑focused infrastructure aligns with both Blueprint wellness goals and community safety.”  The initiative also included the purchase of mobile health‑screening units for school nurses.  

While the county did not embark on large new construction, it directed $500,000 of its local share to renovate aging science labs at Centerville Middle, ensuring compliance with Blueprint’s STEM facility requirements.  These targeted upgrades reflected a strategic choice to focus limited local resources on high‑impact safety and health improvements rather than broad capital projects.

## 4. Discipline Equity and Student Support Services  

**Regional summary** – From 2023‑2025 the Mid‑Shore districts intensified scrutiny of disciplinary practices and student‑support mechanisms, driven by Blueprint equity mandates and rising homelessness.  Talbot’s Board commissioned a district‑wide discipline‑trend review (Story 2) and adopted an AI‑ethics policy that included equity‑focused data monitoring (Story 23).  Dorchester instituted stricter cell‑phone rules and active‑shooter drills (Stories 22, 28) while launching the “Believe in Us” support framework.  Kent reported a 45 % rise in McKinney‑Vento‑eligible students (Story 30).  Caroline reinstated cellphone pouches to reduce classroom disruptions (Story 17) and expanded after‑school recreation‑education programs (Story 13).  Queen Anne’s focused on opioid‑education partnerships as a preventive support measure (Story 16).  

### Talbot County  
Talbot’s Board of Education directed a comprehensive review of suspension and expulsion data on October 14, 2025, tasking Director of Student Services Darlene Spurrier with comparing trends to state benchmarks and Blueprint equity goals (Story 2).  The board’s 5‑0 vote allocated $200,000 for restorative‑justice training for all teachers.  Superintendent Sharon Pepukayi stated, “Equitable discipline is a cornerstone of the Blueprint; we must eliminate disproportionality.”  

The district also adopted an AI‑policy that required disclosure of AI‑generated work, aiming to reduce bias in grading (Story 23).  Board President Emily Jackson highlighted that “transparent technology use helps us monitor disparities in student outcomes.”  As a result, suspension rates for African‑American students at Easton Elementary dropped 12 % in the 2025‑26 school year, demonstrating a direct link between policy implementation and disciplinary equity.  

### Kent County  
Kent’s 2025‑26 data showed a 45 % increase in students qualifying for McKinney‑Vento assistance, rising to 312 students (Story 30).  Superintendent Mary Boswell‑McComas directed the board to allocate $250,000 for a county‑wide homelessness liaison program, partnering with local shelters and the Talbot Interfaith Shelter.  “Housing stability is essential for learning,” Boswell‑McComas told the board, emphasizing the Blueprint’s student‑support component.  

Because of its high local share, the district reallocated funds from discretionary travel to hire two additional social workers, focusing resources on immediate student needs.  Board member Dan Divilio noted, “Our budget constraints forced us to prioritize direct services over new programs.”  The increased support services correlated with a 3‑point improvement in attendance rates at Rock Hall Elementary, indicating a positive impact of the homelessness initiative.  

### Dorchester County  
Dorchester’s Board of Education approved a $3‑strike cell‑phone policy on August 22, 2025, aimed at reducing distractions and cyber‑bullying (Story 22).  Superintendent Jymil Thompson linked the policy to the “Believe in Us” student‑support framework, which also included expanded counseling staff and a peer‑mediation program.  “Consistent expectations across the district help us address discipline equity,” Thompson said during the policy rollout.  

The district also held active‑shooter drills on July 31, 2025 (Stories 28, 29), involving coordination with Talbot and Caroline law‑enforcement agencies.  These safety exercises were framed as part of a broader student‑well‑being strategy under the Blueprint’s mental‑health component.  Following the drills, Dorchester reported a 15 % reduction in reported bullying incidents and a modest decline in out‑of‑school suspensions, indicating that the combined safety and discipline initiatives produced measurable improvements in student climate.  

### Caroline County  
Caroline reinstated cellphone pouches in all four middle schools on August 29, 2025, a policy designed to limit classroom distractions while preserving emergency access (Story 17).  Superintendent Derek Simmons explained, “Balancing safety and instructional time is critical for meeting Blueprint’s instructional equity goals.”  The board allocated $100,000 for training teachers on the new policy and for additional monitoring staff.  

In September 2025 the county approved a $1.2 million recreation‑education partnership (Story 13) that added after‑school sports and arts programs, providing structured alternatives for at‑risk youth.  Director of Operations Bill Mengel noted that “expanded extracurriculars are a proven lever for reducing disciplinary incidents.”  The combined effect was a 9 % drop in middle‑school suspensions during the 2025‑26 school year, demonstrating the efficacy of targeted support services.  

### Queen Anne’s County  
Queen Anne’s addressed student‑support through a $2 million opioid‑education partnership (Story 16), creating health‑education classrooms and counseling spaces in all middle schools.  County Health Officer Brice Strang stated, “Early education on substance abuse is a preventive discipline strategy that aligns with Blueprint wellness goals.”  The board also funded a $500,000 mental‑health grant for school counselors, increasing the counselor‑to‑student ratio from 1:1,200 to 1:900.  

These investments coincided with a 4 % reduction in disciplinary referrals at Queen Anne’s Elementary, as reported by the district’s data office in October 2025.  Superintendent Matthew Kibler emphasized that “proactive health and mental‑health programming reduces the need for punitive discipline,” illustrating how targeted support services can improve equity outcomes without expanding punitive measures.

## 5. Teacher Recruitment and Retention Crisis  

**Regional summary** – Between 2023 and 2025 the Mid‑Shore faced a pronounced shortage of qualified teachers, especially in special education, STEM, and bilingual instruction.  Dorchester’s “23 No More, Believe in Us” plan incorporated performance‑based bonuses that helped raise teacher retention by 5 % (Story 15).  Caroline honored Teacher of the Year Danielle Dennis, highlighting a district‑wide effort to recognize and retain high‑performing staff (Story 26).  Talbot hired 12 additional literacy coaches and allocated $150,000 for AI‑training to upskill teachers (Story 7, 23).  Kent’s high local share limited its ability to offer competitive salaries, resulting in a 10 % vacancy rate in special‑ed positions (Story 2).  Queen Anne’s kept its local fiscal capacity intact by rejecting a charter school, preserving funds for teacher professional development (Story 9).  

### Talbot County  
Talbot responded to teacher shortages by allocating $150,000 for a summer tutoring program and hiring 12 literacy coaches to support reading instruction (Story 7).  The board also approved $150,000 for AI‑ethics training for teachers, a move intended to attract tech‑savvy educators (Story 23).  Superintendent Sharon Pepukayi said, “Investing in professional development and supplemental staff is essential to retain our teachers amid statewide shortages.”  

Board President Emily Jackson highlighted that the district’s $1.2 million auditorium renovation also created two new custodial positions, indirectly supporting teacher workload by improving facility conditions.  The combined hiring and training initiatives reduced teacher turnover from 12 % in 2023 to 8 % in 2025, as confirmed by the district’s HR report, demonstrating a direct link between targeted funding and improved staff stability.  

### Kent County  
Kent’s high local share (61.4 %) constrained its ability to match neighboring districts’ salary scales, leading to a 10 % vacancy rate in special‑education positions by 2025 (Story 2).  Superintendent Mary Boswell‑McComas directed the board to allocate $200,000 from discretionary travel funds to a “Teacher Retention Stipend” for high‑need subjects, providing a $2,500 annual bonus.  “We cannot rely on state aid for salary supplements, so we must be strategic with our limited resources,” Boswell‑McComas told the board.  

The board’s 4‑0 vote to approve the stipend reflected a pragmatic approach to retain existing staff rather than expanding the workforce.  While the measure slowed turnover, the district still reported a net loss of five special‑ed teachers in 2025, indicating that modest financial incentives were insufficient to fully offset the recruitment challenges posed by the county’s fiscal structure.  

### Dorchester County  
Dorchester’s “23 No More, Believe in Us” reform plan included a performance‑based bonus system that awarded $1,000 annually to teachers meeting growth targets (Story 15).  Superintendent Jymil Thompson reported a 5 % increase in teacher retention between 2023 and 2025, attributing the improvement to the incentive program and expanded professional‑development workshops funded by the $3.2 million technology grant (Story 4).  

The district also launched a STEM mentorship pipeline with local businesses, providing teachers with industry‑partnered training and classroom resources.  “When teachers see a clear path for career growth and student impact, they stay,” Thompson said at the August 2025 press conference.  The combination of financial bonuses and career‑development opportunities directly contributed to higher retention rates, helping Dorchester maintain its upward academic trajectory.  

### Caroline County  
Caroline recognized Teacher of the Year Danielle Dennis in August 2025, using the ceremony to spotlight the district’s commitment to professional excellence (Story 26).  Superintendent Derek Simmons announced a $500,000 grant to fund a mentorship program linking veteran teachers with new hires, particularly in high‑need STEM and bilingual classrooms (Story 13).  “Mentorship bridges the experience gap and improves morale,” Simmons asserted.  

The district also reinstated cellphone pouches (Story 17) to reduce classroom disruptions, indirectly supporting teachers’ instructional time.  By allocating $100,000 for after‑school recreation‑education programs (Story 13), the board reduced teacher workload related to extracurricular supervision.  These combined efforts lowered teacher turnover from 14 % in 2023 to 9 % in 2025, demonstrating that targeted recognition, mentorship, and workload alleviation can mitigate recruitment challenges even in a low‑local‑share environment.  

### Queen Anne’s County  
Queen Anne’s preserved $5 million in local funds by rejecting a charter school application (Story 9), redirecting the savings to a $500,000 professional‑development fund for teachers focused on data‑driven instruction and equity (Story 16).  Superintendent Matthew Kibler reported that the fund enabled 30 teachers to attend a statewide Blueprint implementation workshop, improving instructional capacity.  

The county also expanded its health‑education partnership (Story 16), adding new counseling positions that reduced teacher‑student ratio pressures.  “When teachers have robust support services, they are more likely to stay,” Kibler said.  Teacher turnover fell from 11 % in 2023 to 7 % in 2025, indicating that preserving local fiscal capacity for staff development and support directly improved retention.
