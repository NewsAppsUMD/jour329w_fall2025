# Understanding Sports Journalism Through the Lens of Maryland and Regional Coverage

Welcome to sports journalism in the Maryland, D.C., and Virginia region. If you're getting started, you're entering one of the most diverse and complex sports markets in America—a place where professional championships, college athletics, high school competitions, and emerging sports all intersect. This guide will help you understand what matters, who to know, and where opportunities exist for fresh reporting.

## The Landscape: What You're Covering

The DMV (D.C.-Maryland-Virginia) region offers unusual breadth. You have professional teams across all major sports, powerhouse college programs, passionate high school athletics, and increasingly, emerging stories around youth sports, international development, and the business of sports.

### Professional Sports: The Core Beat

The region's professional teams provide consistent news cycles but also complex narratives beyond game recaps. The **Baltimore Orioles** and **Washington Nationals** offer contrasting baseball stories—the Orioles endured a historic post-2019 World Series collapse (a .407 winning percentage over five seasons) while rebuilding through international scouting changes, while the Nationals won their first championship in 2019. Both teams generate stories about franchise direction, player development, and fan engagement.

The **Washington Capitals** won the Stanley Cup in 2018, and their coverage has evolved from "can they win?" to succession planning as Alex Ovechkin ages. The **Baltimore Ravens** remain competitive but face questions about quarterback play and offensive philosophy, particularly regarding fourth-quarter playoff turnovers. The **Washington Commanders** (formerly Washington Football Team) continue navigating ownership controversies and roster rebuilding.

Basketball coverage centers on the **Washington Wizards**, where stars like Bradley Beal and the legacy of John Wall create storylines around playoff disappointment and roster construction. The **Washington Mystics** of the WNBA offer important coverage opportunities often overlooked by mainstream outlets.

**Key institutions to know**: Capital One Arena, Camden Yards, Nationals Park, M&T Bank Stadium, FedEx Field, Audi Field (D.C. United's soccer-specific stadium that opened in 2018).

### College Athletics: Beyond the Big Names

The **University of Maryland** competes in the Big Ten across multiple sports, with football and basketball drawing the most attention. Recent coverage has highlighted attendance struggles (inflated figures through NCAA reporting rules), coaching turnover, and the tragic death of Jordan McNair in 2018, which led to investigations into the football program's culture.

Maryland's lacrosse programs—both men's and women's—are national powers. The men's team won the national championship recently, and coaches like Caitlyn Phipps (who moved to Georgetown) represent important transition stories. Johns Hopkins University maintains one of college lacrosse's historic rivalries with Maryland.

**Georgetown University**, **Howard University** (which made headlines recruiting five-star basketball player Makur Maker), **Navy** (with its distinctive flexbone triple-option offense in football), and various smaller schools provide diverse angles. Don't overlook HBCUs like Morgan State or mid-major programs that produce compelling human interest stories.

### High School Sports: Undercover Goldmines

This is where many of your freshest stories will come from. Maryland high school athletics are deeply followed, particularly in football (DeMatha Catholic, St. Frances Academy) and lacrosse. Recent coverage has explored Name, Image, and Likeness (NIL) deals for high school athletes—a four-month Shirley Povich Center investigation documented how NIL is transforming high school sports, with Maryland being one of 41 states (plus D.C.) allowing such compensation.

**St. Frances Academy** has become a national football powerhouse, fundamentally changing the competitive landscape in Baltimore. This created tensions within the MIAA conference and generates ongoing stories about competitive balance, recruiting, and what it means to be a "local" program.

Athletes like Alexis Ewing (volleyball, first Maryland high schooler to sign an NIL deal with College HUNKS Hauling Junk & Moving), Rodney Rice (basketball star who turned down $600,000 from Overtime Elite), and others represent the intersection of athletics, business, and adolescence that makes for compelling journalism.

### Emerging and Alternative Sports

The region has hosted unique events: the Premier Lacrosse League held a quarantined tournament; the Maryland Black Bears became the state's first junior hockey team in the NAHL; the XFL's D.C. Defenders drew 17,000 fans before the league folded; pickleball has exploded in popularity; and competitive gaming/esports continues growing.

International sports development is another rich area. The Shirley Povich Center produced an extensive documentary series on the NBA Academy Africa in Senegal, exploring how basketball is developing across the continent and the pathway from Dakar to the NBA. This kind of international sports journalism—examining youth development, cultural exchange, and the business of global sports—offers significant opportunities.

## Story Types and Angles: What's Been Done, What's Missing

### Investigations and Explanatory Journalism

The Povich Center has produced substantial investigative work that sets a model:

**NIL and High School Sports**: A four-month investigation examined how Name, Image, and Likeness payments are affecting high school athletics, revealing a patchwork of state policies, questions about transfers and recruiting, and the need for education programs for athletes and parents. The series included profiles of businesses like Portland Gear and College HUNKS using high school athletes as endorsers.

**Youth Tackle Football**: A comprehensive project explored how parental decisions about youth football participation vary by race and geography, finding that Black and Hispanic parents are nearly twice as likely as white parents to view football as a path to college scholarships and the NFL, despite health risks. This included on-the-ground reporting in Lexington, Mississippi (where football remains crucial despite poverty) and suburban Atlanta.

**Sports Betting and Universities**: Coverage examined the University of Colorado's partnership with PointsBet, including how betting companies market to students through social media and event sponsorships, raising ethical questions about gambling access on college campuses.

**Climate Change and Sports**: An analysis of how rising temperatures will affect sports infrastructure (more domed stadiums), schedules (baseball season adjustments), and entire sports communities (ski resorts facing extinction).

**The Jordan McNair Case**: Following the University of Maryland football player's death from heatstroke, reporting documented systemic issues in the program and raised questions about overexertion in college football (22 Division I players have died from overexertion since 2000).

## Sources: Who to Contact and Where to Go

### Building Your Source List

For any story, you need multiple perspectives:

**Official sources**: Coaches, athletic directors, team PR representatives, conference officials. These are usually accessible but will give you managed, on-message quotes. Press them with specific questions backed by data.

**Athletes**: Current and former players offer different perspectives. Recent alumni are often more honest than current players. Look for athletes who've demonstrated willingness to speak on complex issues (John Wall talking about defense and work ethic, for example).

**Experts**: Academic researchers, sports economists, sports psychologists, athletic trainers, scouts, agents. These sources provide context and analysis that elevates stories beyond simple game recaps.

**Community members**: Parents, youth coaches, fans, local business owners near stadiums. They're affected by sports but rarely quoted.

**Documents and data**: Game statistics, financial records (for public universities), injury reports, recruiting rankings, attendance figures. Always verify what officials tell you against available data.

## What to Avoid

### Common Pitfalls

**Over-reliance on press releases and official statements**: Your job is to go beyond what teams want you to know. Press releases are starting points, not stories.

**Treating all sports as game recaps**: Unless you witness something truly unusual, game recaps are commodity journalism. Focus on trends, profiles, investigations, and explanatory pieces.

**Ignoring smaller sports and lower levels**: Some of the best stories come from high school track meets or college club rugby teams. Elite sports are covered by everyone; finding stories others miss makes your work valuable.

**Assuming everyone follows sports closely**: Explain terms, provide context, and remember that many readers aren't die-hard fans but are interested in sports as human drama.

**Both-sides-ism on issues with factual components**: On questions like whether concussions cause brain damage or whether COVID-19 transmission risk varies indoors vs. outdoors, scientific consensus exists. Giving equal weight to discredited positions doesn't serve readers.

**Missing the money**: Sports are business. Always ask about costs, budgets, revenue, salaries. Who profits? Who pays? Who's excluded by price?

**Neglecting women's sports**: The WNBA's Mystics, Maryland women's basketball and lacrosse, women's soccer—these deserve coverage equal in quality (if not quantity) to men's sports. The excuse that "readers don't care" becomes self-fulfilling.

**Forgetting about race and class**: Sports reflect society's inequalities and occasionally challenge them. Stories about youth football participation varying by race or international players navigating American culture shouldn't feel like "special" coverage—they're fundamental to understanding sports.

**Treating athletes as characters rather than people**: Athletes are workers, students, and individuals navigating complex systems. Avoid reducing them to stats or storylines without recognizing their humanity.

### Specific Warnings

**NIL coverage**: Don't overstate how much high school athletes are earning (most deals are small) or assume all families want their children pursuing NIL opportunities. The 2025 polling data showed support for NIL but limited actual participation.

**Injuries and medical information**: Be extremely careful with information about player injuries, especially concussions. HIPAA protections apply, and pressuring young athletes or their families for medical details is unethical.

**Youth sports**: When covering minors, get parental consent for interviews and be cautious about publishing information that could affect their recruitment or reputations. Consider whether naming a 15-year-old who made a mistake on the field serves any purpose.

**Gambling content**: When writing about sports betting, include responsible gambling resources and be mindful that gambling addiction is serious. The 2022-2023 stories on Maryland's sports betting rollout and university partnerships noted concerns that remain relevant.

**Unverified recruiting information**: High school recruiting is full of rumors. Don't report a player's college commitment until it's officially announced, and be skeptical of recruiting rankings that can be influenced by payment to scouting services.

## How to Move Forward

### Building on Past Coverage

The Shirley Povich Center has established strong precedents for in-depth, analytical sports journalism. Your work should:

**Use data to support reporting**: The best stories in your materials combined on-the-ground reporting with statistical analysis—John Wall's MVP case, the Orioles' walk rate and home run strategy, NFL teams' post-relocation performance. Learn basic statistical analysis and don't be afraid to create your own datasets.

**Take time with investigations**: The NIL series and youth football project both involved months of reporting across multiple locations. Ambitious journalism requires patience and planning.

**Find the human element in systems stories**: The NBA Academy Africa series worked because it profiled individual players navigating the system, not just the system itself. Policy and business stories need people.

**Think multimedia**: The Africa basketball project included video, the youth football project included a documentary. Plan from the start how your story might work across platforms.

**Consider impact**: The Shirley Povich Center's polling work (on national anthems, youth sports, NIL) helped establish what public opinion actually is rather than what Twitter suggests it is. Original survey work or FOIA requests can generate news.

### Developing Your Beat

If you're covering this region regularly:

**Cultivate sources over time**: Send occasional check-in texts to coaches or administrators even when you don't need anything. Attend their events. Show that you care about their programs beyond deadline needs.

**Specialize but stay broad**: Become expert in one area (college basketball recruiting, sports analytics, youth baseball economics) while maintaining general knowledge. Specialization makes you valuable; breadth makes you flexible.

**Read widely**: Follow national sports journalists but also read The Diamondback (Maryland's student newspaper), Baltimore sports radio discussions, local blogs. See what stories they're missing.

**Build institutional knowledge**: Keep a detailed file of contacts, key facts about teams and programs, and story ideas. When you read that Pimlico is undergoing renovation, note when it's supposed to be completed so you can follow up.

**Find your voice**: The "WAR on Hot Takes" podcast worked because Dylan Sinn and Adam Zielonka had a clear analytical perspective. What's yours? What questions do you consistently ask? What assumptions do you challenge?

### Story Development Process

For any story, particularly enterprise pieces:

1. **Identify the question**: Not just "what happened" but "why does this matter?" and "what does this tell us about sports, community, or society?"

2. **Research before reporting**: Read everything available on your topic, understand the data, know what's already been said. The best interviews come when you're already informed.

3. **Report in layers**: Start with official sources and public information, then move to harder-to-reach sources and specific examples. Your story gets better as you go deeper.

4. **Follow the thread**: If an interview source mentions something interesting but tangential, explore it. Some of the best stories come from unexpected directions.

5. **Be persistent but respectful**: If someone says "no comment," ask why and whether there's someone else who can help. Most sources appreciate reporters who do homework and are professional.

6. **Verify aggressively**: Check every fact, especially names, dates, statistics, and quotes. Trust but verify what even reliable sources tell you.

7. **Write with authority**: If you've done the reporting, write confidently. Use strong verbs, specific details, and clear explanations. Avoid hedging unless genuine uncertainty exists.

## Final Thoughts

The DMV sports landscape offers extraordinary variety. You can write about high school athletes navigating NIL deals one day and analyze the Capitals' playoff chances the next. You can profile Paralympic hopefuls or investigate university sports betting partnerships. You can document how basketball develops in Senegal or examine why pickleball has exploded in Montgomery County.

The best sports journalism reveals something about human nature, community dynamics, or societal structures. A story about John Wall mentoring De'Aaron Fox isn't just about basketball; it's about mentorship and professionalism. A story about youth football participation isn't just about a sport; it's about race, class, opportunity, and how parents make impossible choices about their children's futures.

Your advantage as a developing journalist is that you can ask questions others assume they know the answers to. Why do Maryland basketball games have empty seats despite being in a major conference? Why do some NFL teams keep signing mediocre quarterbacks to huge contracts? Why did one family let their son play tackle football while another didn't?

Stay curious. Do the work. Tell true stories. And remember that behind every statistic is a person, behind every program is a community, and behind every game is something worth understanding.
